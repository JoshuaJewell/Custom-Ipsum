The cartographer (a patient man) drew the coast (every inlet and headland) by hand. His apprentice (younger, and rather impatient) mixed the inks (umber, indigo, a little gold) each morning. The map (unfinished for years) covered the table (an oak slab worn smooth). Travellers (merchants mostly, though sometimes a pilgrim) paid well for a copy. One winter (the coldest in memory) a captain (newly returned from the south) brought word of an island (uncharted, or so he claimed). The cartographer (sceptical by trade) listened anyway. The captain (lowering his voice) described a harbour (deep, and sheltered from the wind) ringed by cliffs (white, like bone). The apprentice (forgetting his manners) interrupted twice. The old man (raising one hand) bid him wait. By spring (the inks long dry) the new coast (tentative, dotted where the captain had been unsure) joined the others. The guild (jealous of its charts) demanded the original. The cartographer (who trusted no one) kept a copy (folded small, hidden in the lining of his coat). Years later (the apprentice now a master himself) the island appeared (exactly where the captain had sworn) on every chart in the city.
